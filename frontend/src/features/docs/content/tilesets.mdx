## Split tilesets by construction rules.

A tileset is a working palette, not every environment asset from the same game. Keep tiles together when they share a grid, placement rules, and a purpose. Split them when those rules change.

## Decide what belongs together

### Share a grid, boundaries, and placement rules

Floor, wall, and edge tiles belong in the same tileset when they snap to the same grid, describe the same scale, and share collision and rotation rules. For example, a 16 x 16 grass tile can form one terrain tileset with its center, straight edge, outer corner, inner corner, cliff, and water-edge variants. Keep every variant needed for a continuous boundary together; otherwise, the pieces needed to complete a shape become hard to find.

### Split by purpose and material boundaries

Terrain, architecture, and interior assets can share a visual style while serving different construction tasks, so they work better as separate tilesets: terrain for outdoor ground, architecture for walls, roofs, and exterior structure, and interiors for floors, furniture, and fixtures. Keep repeatable ground, walls, and borders in a base tileset; move flowers, cracks, signs, and other optional details into a decoration tileset so the base layout stays separate from visual variation. Grass, snow, desert, and cave tiles can also be split by material when each tileset has a complete set of centers, edges, and corners. When two materials must meet directly, keep their transition tiles with the matching boundary pieces.

### Keep multi-tile items intact

Keep a bed, tree, shopfront, or large rock as one named item that occupies several grid cells. Do not split it into unrelated pieces unless each piece must be placed independently. A complete item preserves its silhouette and avoids accidental gaps when it is moved or edited.

## Use a simple split check

Before creating a new tileset, ask:

- Do these tiles work together to build the same part of a scene?
- Do they use the same grid size, scale, collision, and rotation rules?
- Are all neighboring edges, corners, and material transitions available in the same tileset?
- Can optional decoration be removed without breaking the map's readable structure?

If the answer is yes to most of these questions, keep the tiles together. If they require different construction steps or placement rules, create separate tilesets.
